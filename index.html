The Wheel of This Model

Six spokes · cyan = Group A · fuchsia = Group B · a fair model draws one wheel, not two
What is this wheel? What do the spokes mean?

The Dharma Wheel of Fairness is a radar chart: each spoke is one performance metric of your trained model, drawn separately per group — cyan for Group A, fuchsia for Group B. Same model, same rules; the only difference is who is being judged. Outward is always better.

SELECTSelection rate — what fraction of the group receives the positive outcome (released, approved, shortlisted).
TPRTrue Positive Rate — of the genuinely qualified, how many the model correctly approves. Also called recall, or equal opportunity.
1−FPRInverse False Positive Rate — how well the model avoids wrongly approving the unqualified (inverted so outward = better).
PRECPrecision — of everyone approved, how many actually deserved it.
NPVNegative Predictive Value — of everyone rejected, how many actually deserved rejection.
ACCAccuracy — overall fraction of correct verdicts for that group.

Why it matters: a perfectly fair model draws one wheel, not two — the polygons would overlap exactly. Every gap between the shapes is a specific, named kind of unfairness. And the spokes fight each other: equalise TPR with group thresholds and precision drifts apart. That is Chamber V's impossibility theorem made visible. The wheel only turns true when every spoke is equal — and this sandbox lets you feel why that is so hard.

V

The Impossible Wheel

Theorem · Interactive

Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan proved it in 2016: when two groups have different base rates, no classifier can equalise calibration, false-positive rates, and false-negative rates at once. This was the heart of the COMPAS debate — both sides were right about their own metric. Slide the threshold and try to light all three lamps green. You can't. That's the theorem.

One Threshold, Three Demands

Set to 0% and equality becomes possible — the theorem only bites when the world itself is unequal.
